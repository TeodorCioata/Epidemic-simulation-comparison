# RESULTS

Every number comes from the final run
(`python run_all.py`, seed 113, 100 replicates per configuration). Every table is given in Markdown and
in LaTeX; `results/tables.md` holds the same tables, regenerated by `python run_all.py tables`. The LaTeX
compiles with pdflatex given `\usepackage[utf8]{inputenc}`, `[T1]{fontenc}` and `{booktabs}`; the wide
tables (RQ1, RQ2, immunisation) need `\small` and `\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{...}` or a landscape page.
Figures are in `docs/figures/` as PNG and PDF.

Notation: M1 has rate $\tau$ on every edge; M2 has $\tau_{ij} = c\,w_{ij}^{\alpha}$ with mean weight 1.
A *difference* is the median over replicates of the paired $(M2 - M1)/M1$, in %, with a 95 % bootstrap
interval in brackets.

## 0 Setting: parameters and the M1 baseline

The disease rates are main.py's. Each configuration has 100 replicates: 100 graphs, each with its own
initial infected and random stream. The baseline table describes M1 alone, the reference every
difference below is measured against. Barabási–Albert spreads fastest ($R_0$ 6.27, analytic $r$ 2.12)
but infects the fewest (attack rate 90.4 %): its hubs are reached early, its many degree-3 nodes
less often. About one infected person in eleven dies, $\delta/(\gamma + \delta) = 0.091$; the
simulated death fractions (8.2–8.7 % of all nodes) are that share of the attack rate.

## 1 RQ1: the matching protocol (`rq1.png`)

**Shows.** M2 against M1 on E, C and B (rows) for peak prevalence, peak time, attack rate and the
simulated growth rate $\hat r$ (columns), against the weight dispersion $CV_w \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 2\}$
(iid gamma weights, $\alpha = 1$); one line per protocol, 95 % bootstrap bars.

**Key numbers** (table below; Erdős–Rényi at $CV_w = 2$ unless stated):

- **(i) equal mean rate** lowers M2's $R_0$ from 3.33 to 1.84 and changes every metric: peak
  prevalence −56.2 % [−57.4, −55.3], peak time +83.7 % [79.3, 93.2], attack rate −22.2 %
  [−22.5, −21.8], $\hat r$ −53.1 % [−55.5, −47.7]. M2 also takes off less often: P(major) 0.88 against
  1.00 (Barabási–Albert: 0.78 against 0.95, attack rate −32.8 % [−33.3, −32.2]).
- **(ii) equal $R_0$** keeps the attack rate (0.0 % [−0.1, 0.1]; within 0.4 % in every cell and network)
  but M2 is faster: analytic $r$ 1.15 → 1.61, $\hat r$ +37.5 % [32.2, 43.4], peak 25.0 % earlier
  [−26.7, −23.7] and 14.9 % higher [13.7, 16.4]; the epidemic ends 12.0 % sooner [−15.1, −9.2]
  (duration, in `results/rq1/summary.csv`).
- **(iii) equal growth rate** keeps the timing (peak time −1.7 % [−5.1, 1.4], $\hat r$ −4.2 %
  [−6.9, 2.0]) but needs a smaller $R_0$ (2.74): attack rate −4.1 % [−4.2, −4.0], peak −7.6 %
  [−8.7, −6.2]. On Barabási–Albert the cost in size is three times larger: attack rate −12.4 %
  [−12.8, −11.9], peak −17.8 % [−19.9, −16.4].
- The configuration model behaves like Erdős–Rényi. At $CV_w = 0.5$ every difference under (ii) and
  (iii) is within 3.7 % ($\hat r$ within 5.4 %), and at $CV_w = 0$ every difference is exactly 0.

**Takeaway.** No single scale $c$ makes M2 equal to M1: matching $R_0$ fixes the final size only,
matching the growth rate fixes the timing only, and matching the mean rate moves everything because $T$
is concave in $\tau$.

**Caveats.** $\hat r$ is the noisiest metric (fitted between the 25th and 250th infection; its
intervals are up to ±17 % wide on Barabási–Albert). Under (i) fewer pairs enter the comparison at $CV_w = 2$
(88 on E, 77 on B), because M2 takes off less often. Mean degree is 7.0 on E and C but 6.0 on B
(main.py's $m = \lfloor 7/2 \rfloor$), so compare M2 with M1 within a network, not across networks.

## 2 RQ2: weight–degree correlation (`theta.png`)

**Shows.** M2 with $w_{ij} \propto (k_i k_j)^\theta$ against M1 on E and B, for
$\theta \in \{-0.5, 0, 0.5, 1\}$, under protocols (ii) and (iii) (solid). Dashed with open markers: the
*shuffle null*, the same weights permuted at random across edges. It has exactly the same $CV_w$ and
weight distribution but no correlation with degree (Spearman $\rho$ 0.00 instead of ±1), so the gap
between a solid and a dashed line is the effect of the correlation alone.

**The prediction tested.** With weights independent of the structure, equal $R_0$ implies an equal
final size, because in a large tree-like graph both then depend on the transmissibilities only
through $\langle T\rangle$ (RQ1: within 0.4 %). Weight–degree correlation should break this.

**Key numbers** (protocol (ii); table below):

- The prediction holds for every shuffle null: attack rate within 0.1 % of M1 on both networks
  (e.g. B, $\theta = 1$: −0.0 % [−0.2, 0.2]).
- With the correlation it breaks, and message passing predicts by how much (theory column).
  On Erdős–Rényi the attack rate changes by +2.3 % [2.2, 2.4] ($\theta = -0.5$), −4.0 %
  [−4.1, −3.8] ($\theta = 0.5$) and −9.0 % [−9.1, −8.8] ($\theta = 1$). On Barabási–Albert, by +10.1 %
  [9.9, 10.4], −51.7 % [−52.5, −50.8] and −80.8 % [−82.2, −80.0] (theory: +10.1, −52.2, −81.0).
- On Barabási–Albert with $\theta > 0$ M2 also takes off far less often: P(major) 0.63 ($\theta = 0.5$)
  and 0.26 ($\theta = 1$) against 0.95. Hub–hub edges carry the weight; matching $R_0$ then makes the
  many other edges weak, and the epidemic stays in the hub core.
- $\theta < 0$ does the opposite (weight on low-degree contacts): on B the peak is 47.3 % higher
  [45.5, 49.0] and 28.5 % earlier [−30.6, −24.3].
- Under (iii) the pattern is the same; there, even the shuffle null on B at $\theta = 1$ loses 6.7 %
  [−7.1, −6.2]: that is the RQ1 effect of a large dispersion ($CV_w = 3.98$), not of the correlation.

**Takeaway.** Matching $R_0$ guarantees an equal final size only when weights are unrelated to degree;
weight–degree correlation changes the final size at fixed $R_0$, strongly on a heavy-tailed network.

**Caveats.** $\theta$ also sets the dispersion, and differently per network: $CV_w$ is 0.28, 0.24 and 0.48
on E but 0.52, 1.01 and 3.98 on B. Compare each correlated cell with its own shuffle null, not with
another $\theta$. At $\theta = 1$ on B only 26 (ii) and 14 (iii) pairs enter, so its timing intervals are
wide.

## 3 RQ2: the exponent α (`alpha.png`)

**Shows.** M2 with iid weights at $CV_w = 1$ and rates $c\,w^\alpha$, for $\alpha \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 2\}$, on
E and B under (ii) and (iii). $\alpha$ sets how strongly contact strength turns into risk; $\alpha = 0$ is
M1.

**Key numbers** (table below): at $\alpha = 0$ every difference is exactly 0. Under (ii) the attack rate
stays matched (within 0.4 %), while the timing effects grow with $\alpha$. On Erdős–Rényi the peak comes
2.4 % [−4.5, 0.5], 9.2 % [−11.0, −7.5] and 19.6 % [−22.2, −18.4] earlier for $\alpha = 0.5, 1, 2$, and
$\hat r$ rises by up to 31.6 % [26.1, 39.2]. Under (iii) the timing stays matched and the size cost grows:
attack rate −0.5 %, −1.3 %, −3.1 % on E and −1.3 %, −4.2 %, −9.9 % on B.

**Takeaway.** With independent weights, $\alpha$ acts as a dial on rate dispersion, the RQ1 axis: it
leaves the final size alone under equal $R_0$ and moves everything else, more on the heavy-tailed
network.

**Caveats.** $\alpha$ and $CV_w$ are not interchangeable. $w^2$ for $CV_w = 1$ is more dispersed than
$CV_w = 2$ at $\alpha = 1$, yet its effects are smaller (E, (ii), peak time −19.6 % against −25.0 %):
the shape of the rate distribution matters, not only its spread. The $\alpha = 1$ cells are the RQ1
cells at $CV_w = 1$ (same seed, same medians).

## 4 RQ2: BBV weights (table)

**Shows.** The BBV network (W): grown by strength-preferential attachment, with the weights of a
target's edges reinforced at each step (reinforcement 1, $m = 3$). M1 runs on the BBV graph with equal
rates, M2 with BBV's own weights, and the shuffle null with those weights permuted. Reinforcement also
shapes the topology (M1 $R_0$ = 10.57 here, against 6.27 on B), so compare M2 with M1 on W only.

**Key numbers** (table below): BBV weights are both dispersed ($CV_w$ = 1.33) and correlated with degree
(Spearman $\rho$ = 0.49). Under (ii) the attack rate falls by 29.9 % [−31.2, −28.0] (theory −30.0) and the
peak by 35.4 % [−38.9, −33.5], and M2 takes off less often (P(major) 0.80 against 0.94). The shuffle
null keeps the attack rate (−0.1 % [−0.7, 0.4]). Under (iii) the attack rate falls by 52.0 %
[−52.5, −50.6] (shuffle null: −2.3 % [−2.8, −1.5]).

**Takeaway.** Weights that grow with the network behave like positive weight–degree correlation:
at equal $R_0$ they make the epidemic markedly smaller, and the correlation, not the dispersion, is
responsible.

**Caveats.** One reinforcement value. $\hat r$ is very noisy on W ($R_0$ above 10: growth is over within
a few generations), so the timing columns carry little weight.

## 5 Countermeasures (`countermeasures.png`)

**Shows.** The question: does the ranking of immunisation strategies survive the move
from M1 to M2? Before seeding, 5, 10 or 20 % of the nodes are immunised (they start in R and are never
seeded) by one of four strategies:
- **random**;
- **degree**: the most contacts;
- **strength**: the largest $s_i = \sum_j w_{ij}$;
- **acquaintance**: a random neighbour of a random node.

Setting: protocol (ii), iid weights with $CV_w = 2$, $\alpha = 1$, on E and B. Random, degree and
acquaintance immunise exactly the same nodes in M1 and M2; in M1 strength equals degree, so those two
coincide (checked: identical runs). The figure shows the reduction of the expected attack rate and peak
against no intervention, M1 solid and M2 dashed.

**Key numbers** (tables below):

- **The ranking survives.** In both models: degree > strength > acquaintance > random, with degree
  and strength tied in M1, on Barabási–Albert at every coverage and on Erdős–Rényi at 20 %. On E at 5
  and 10 % random and acquaintance immunisation are statistically tied: random comes first in both
  models at 5 %, and at 10 % the two swap between M1 and M2 within overlapping intervals (M2: 10.0 %
  [7.1, 11.8] against 11.7 % [9.8, 12.6]).
- **What changes is the gap between degree and strength.** Strength targeting is as good as degree
  targeting in M1 by construction, but worse in M2, where a node's strength is dominated by a few
  strong contacts rather than by its number of contacts. On Barabási–Albert at 10 %: degree 81.0 %
  [77.4, 84.2] against strength 55.4 % [51.1, 60.1] (attack rate), and outbreaks still take off in
  79 % of runs after strength targeting against 54 % after degree targeting. On E the gap is clear
  for the peak (20 %: 63.8 % [61.8, 65.9] against 56.6 % [54.2, 59.1]) but within the intervals for
  the attack rate (36.1 % [32.7, 39.6] against 33.3 % [29.8, 37.0]).
- Degree targeting at 20 % prevents every outbreak on B (P(major) 0.00, reduction 99.8 % in both
  models).
- **Edge measures (M2 only).** Halving the rate of the strongest 10 % of contacts barely helps (attack
  rate −0.1 % on E, −0.2 % on B): those contacts transmit almost surely, halved or not. Removing 10 % of
  contacts at random reduces the attack rate by 2.0 % [−2.2, 5.9] on E and 4.3 % [4.1, 4.4] on B, and
  the peak by 9.0 % and 8.8 %.

**Takeaway.** The order of the strategies is the same in both model classes, but the weighted model
separates degree from strength targeting: knowing who has many contacts beats knowing who has
intense ones, and reducing the intensity of already-intense contacts is nearly useless.

**Caveats.** Independent weights only; with weights that follow degree (RQ2) strength and degree
targeting would pick more similar nodes. One protocol, one dispersion. The edge measures are not
budget-matched, and the mismatch favours halving: at $CV_w = 2$ the strongest 10 % of contacts carry
about 58 % of the total rate, so halving them removes about 29 % of all transmission rate, against
about 10 % for random removal. Halving still helps less, so the conclusion holds with even greater certainty.

### 6.6 Simulator checks (`pytest`)

M2 with equal weights or $\alpha = 0$ reproduces M1 event for event. On two nodes the infection
frequency equals $T$. On three nodes with waning the simulator matches the exactly solvable Markov
chain. Deaths and infections agree with `main.py`, whose waning now also matches that
chain. The test suite has 113 tests and runs in under a minute.


## Methods, in short

**Simulator.** A continuous-time Markov SEIR+D model, simulated event by event (no time step). A
priority queue holds future events `(time, node, new state)`; the earliest is applied if the node is
still in the state it expects, otherwise it is dropped as stale. When node $i$ becomes infectious it
draws its exit, recovery at rate $\gamma$ or death at rate $\delta$ (whichever exponential clock rings
first), and for each susceptible neighbour $j$ a contact time at rate $\tau_{ij}$; the contact is
scheduled only if it comes before the exit. Latency is exponential at rate $\sigma$; a rate of 0 means
"never". Immunised nodes start in R. The output is the initial states plus the event log; every metric,
curve and picture is a replay of it. Cost: $O(\log N)$ per event, about 0.1 s per epidemic at
$N = 5000$. Checks: two nodes infect with probability $T$; a three-node waning chain matches the exact
Markov chain; M2 with equal weights reproduces M1 event for event; deaths and infections agree with
`main.py`; attack-rate distributions agree with EoN (validation section).

**$R_0$ from the non-backtracking matrix.** With $T_{ij} = \tau_{ij}/(\tau_{ij} + \gamma + \delta)$, the
probability that $i$ infects $j$ before leaving I, $R_0 = \rho(B)$, the spectral radius of
$B_{(i\to j),(j\to l)} = T_{jl}$ for $l \ne i$: infection paths along directed edges that never step
straight back. It is exact for tree-like graphs whatever their degree correlations and whatever the
pattern of $T_{ij}$, and it is the quantity protocol (ii) matches. Newman's
$R_0 = \langle T\rangle(\langle k^2\rangle - \langle k\rangle)/\langle k\rangle$ assumes no degree
correlations and weights independent of degree; it is reported as a diagnostic only, because it misses
by 18–30 % on Barabási–Albert graphs and by far more under weight–degree correlation.

**Protocol (iii) and why it is computed, not fitted.** The early growth rate $r$ is computed from the network Euler–Lotka
condition $\rho(B(r)) = 1$, where $B(r)$ uses the Laplace transform of the infection-to-transmission
time,
$T_r = \frac{\sigma}{\sigma + r}\cdot\frac{\tau_{ij}}{\tau_{ij} + \gamma + \delta + r}$ (so $T_0 = T$ and
$r = 0$ exactly at $R_0 = 1$). M2's scale $c$ is then chosen so that $\rho(B(r^*)) = 1$ at M1's $r^*$.
Reasons: a fit needs tens of simulations per step of the root-finder and gives a noisy target, while the
analytic $r$ is exact on tree-like graphs and costs one root-find. The simulated growth rate $\hat r$
(slope of log cumulative incidence between the 25th and the 250th infection) is kept as an
independent check, and agrees (validation).

**Final size by message passing.** $u_{i\leftarrow j} = 1 - T_{ij} + T_{ij}\prod_{l \in N(j)\setminus i}
u_{j\leftarrow l}$, iterated to a fixed point, and attack rate $= 1 - \frac1N\sum_i \prod_{j\in N(i)}
u_{i\leftarrow j}$. Transmissions *into* a node come from different infectious periods and are
independent, so this is exact for the size of a major outbreak on a tree-like graph, for any $T_{ij}$.
(The correlation among one node's *outgoing* transmissions only affects the outbreak probability.)
It is the "attack rate, theory" column of the RQ2 tables.

**Paired design and intervals.** In each replicate one graph, one set of initial infected (2 random
nodes) and one random stream for the dynamics are shared: M1 runs once and M2 once per cell (common
random numbers). The outcome per replicate is the relative difference $(M2 - M1)/M1$; a cell reports
its median over replicates and a 95 % percentile bootstrap interval of that median (2000 resamples).
The CSVs are bit-for-bit reproducible from the seed, whatever the number of worker processes.

**Conditioning on major outbreaks.** A run is a major outbreak if at least 5 % of the nodes are ever
infected. Differences are computed over the replicates in which both M1 and M2 are major; the
probability of a major outbreak is reported for each model. Countermeasures are the exception: a good
strategy often stops the outbreak, so they use the *expected burden*, $1 - \overline{X}_{\text{with}} /
\overline{X}_{\text{without}}$ over all replicates (extinct runs included), with a paired bootstrap
over replicates.
